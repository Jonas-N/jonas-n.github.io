Research

Why do communication systems take the shape they do? I look for causal answers: what changes in the environment, the interaction or the body, and what then changes in the signal. I approach this from three directions that increasingly meet.

{{ faces.title }}

Faces are usually studied as displays of emotion. In face-to-face conversation they do much more: they mark questions and doubt, signal acceptance or rejection, and change how the words they accompany are understood. From 2020 to 2024 I worked on the ERC project FACESYNTAX with Rachael Jack, Philippe Schyns and colleagues at the University of Glasgow, which treats facial movement as a structured communicative signal.

We use a generative model of 3D facial behaviour and reverse correlation: listeners judge many randomly animated faces, and their responses reveal which movements drive a given inference. Holding speech constant, specific upper-face movements shift whether a speaker comes across as confident or doubtful, and these mappings are largely shared by British English and Mandarin Chinese listeners. Other work identifies an expansion–contraction dimension of facial movement that signals acceptance versus rejection, and traces pragmatic and semantic facial signals back to biologically rooted expressions.

At the Centre for Language Evolution Studies in Toruń, Chen Zhou, Przemysław Żywiczyński and I have proposed a field-based framework for modelling how multimodal signals take part in value-governed exchange in social interaction.

{{ vr.title }}

Experiments on communication face a trade-off: tight control usually means stripping away the live, embodied interaction that communication depends on. Immersive virtual reality removes much of that trade-off. It lets me repeat a manipulation exactly while people move, point, look and talk freely in a shared space.

My PhD at the Centre for Language Evolution in Edinburgh, supervised by Kenny Smith, Simon Kirby and Jennifer Culbertson, was among the first in the language sciences to use networked, multi-participant VR. I built communication games in Unity in which two people share the same virtual environment, and manipulated that environment to test whether the physical world causally shapes spatial language. The code for the paradigm is on GitHub.

With Kristian Tylén, Riccardo Fusaroli and colleagues in Aarhus, I contributed to a VR cultural-transmission study that followed six simulated generations and found that city layout shapes spatial language, sketch maps and navigation success. I have also written practical guides to VR for language evolution and for language and cognition research, with David Peeters, and run hands-on VR workshops in Glasgow and Toruń.

{{ em.title }}

This has been my focus from the start, first as a student assistant to Kristian Tylén and Riccardo Fusaroli in Aarhus and then on a seed-funded project with them. When pairs of people have to communicate without a shared language, using silent gesture for example, they quickly invent conventions. Whether those conventions become systematic depends jointly on the structure of the things people need to talk about and on the pressures of the interaction itself.

The same logic applies to spatial language. In a collaborative maze task, pairs settled on different ways of describing locations depending on the layout they shared. With Michael Spranger I argued for combining cross-linguistic observation, laboratory interventions and computational models to move from correlation to cause in explaining why languages differ in how they talk about space. With Stefan Hartmann, Peeter Tinits and others I have also used iterated learning to study how usage context shapes grammatical complexity.

In Toruń, with Przemysław Żywiczyński and colleagues, I use motion capture to study how bodies shape new communication systems. How people are physically arranged relative to one another acts as a micro-ecological constraint on the pantomime and gesture systems they develop.
