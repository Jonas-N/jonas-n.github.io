<h1 class="page-title">Research</h1>
<p class="lede">
  I ask how communication systems take the shape they do. The answers I look for are causal: what changes in the environment, the interaction, or the body, and what changes in the signal that follows.
</p>

<h2>Environments that shape language</h2>
<p>
  A long line of the work tests whether linguistic structure adapts to the setting it is used in.
  In silent-gesture experiments, the structure of a new communication system tracked the structure of the referents people had to talk about
  (<a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2018.08.014">Nölle, Staib, Fusaroli &amp; Tylén, 2018, <em>Cognition</em></a>).
  In a collaborative maze, pairs settled on different spatial descriptions depending on the layout they shared
  (<a href="https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-020-0404-9">Nölle, Fusaroli, Mills &amp; Tylén, 2020</a>).
  My <a href="https://era.ed.ac.uk/handle/1842/38890">PhD thesis</a> took that question into virtual reality, with two people sharing a space and having to agree on how to talk about it.
  A recent cultural-transmission study in VR asks the same kind of question of cities: grid-like and landmark-rich street plans leave different traces in route descriptions and sketch maps
  (<a href="https://doi.org/10.1162/OPMI.a.380">Alinam, Fusaroli, Nölle, Vesper &amp; Tylén, 2026, <em>Open Mind</em></a>).
  With Michael Spranger I set out a programme that combines cross-linguistic observation, laboratory experiments, and models
  (<a href="https://doi.org/10.1515/lingvan-2020-0007">Nölle &amp; Spranger, 2022</a>).
</p>

<h2>Faces as part of language</h2>
<p>
  From 2020 to 2024 I worked on the FACESYNTAX project with Rachael Jack, Philippe Schyns, and colleagues in Glasgow.
  The project treats facial movement as a structured communicative signal.
  We have used data-driven psychophysics to ask which facial movements carry iconic information, such as rejection and acceptance, and which ones shift how confident a speaker sounds.
  A current preprint reports that dynamic facial movements change how confident speakers appear across cultures
  (<a href="https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/8hqy9_v1">Nölle, Wu, Arias Sarah, Garrod, Schyns &amp; Jack, 2026</a>).
</p>

<h2>Virtual reality as a method</h2>
<p>
  Much of this work needed two people to share a controllable world.
  I build those experiments in Unity, and I have written practical guides to using VR for language evolution
  (<a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780192886491.013.5">Nölle &amp; Peeters, 2025</a>, Oxford Handbook)
  and for language and cognition more broadly
  (<a href="https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/s2xdp_v1">Nölle, Titus, Zhou &amp; Peeters, forthcoming</a>, with a public preprint).
  Code from the thesis paradigm is on <a href="https://github.com/Jonas-N/OrbHuntSetup">GitHub</a>.
</p>

<h2>Community</h2>

  I am a General Editor of <a href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-and-cognition/"><em>Language and Cognition</em></a>
  and a Handling Editor of <em>Cognitive Processing</em>.
  I chaired the scientific committee of EVOLANG XV and co-edited the proceedings of EVOLANG XV and XVI.
  With Stefan Hartmann and Peeter Tinits I guest-edited two special issues of <em>Language Dynamics and Change</em> and wrote the survey that introduces them
  (<a href="https://doi.org/10.1163/22105832-bja10005">Nölle, Hartmann &amp; Tinits, 2020</a>).
  I am also a co-author of <a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/jole/lzaa001">CHIELD</a>, a database of causal hypotheses in evolutionary linguistics.
</p>
